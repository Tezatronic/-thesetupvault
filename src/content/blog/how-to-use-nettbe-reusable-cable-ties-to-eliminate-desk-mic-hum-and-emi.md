The quickest way to stop that annoying desk‑mic hum is to keep AC power cords physically separate from your microphone, speaker, and video cables, and the easiest tool for doing that is a set of reusable hook‑and‑loop cable ties like Nettbe.

In a typical home‑office setup the power strip, laptop charger, and monitor adapters sit right next to the audio interface, desk‑mic stand, and speaker wires. That proximity creates a magnetic field that rides up the unshielded audio conductors and shows up as a low‑frequency buzz in recordings or during video calls. By routing power and audio cables on different planes, using a dedicated tie‑down system, and securing them with a reliable, adjustable fastener, you can dramatically reduce electromagnetic interference (EMI) without buying expensive shielded cables.

Below is a practical, hands‑on guide that walks you through the whole process—from planning your cable routes to tightening the final bundle with Nettbe reusable cable ties. The steps work for a single‑mic podcast rig, a dual‑monitor streaming workstation, or any hybrid setup where power and audio share a desk.

## 1. Map Your Cable Landscape

Before you reach for any tie, take a quick inventory of what’s on your desk:

| Category | Typical Items | Why It Matters |
|----------|---------------|----------------|
| **Power** | Wall adapter, monitor power cords, laptop charger, USB‑C PD hub | Carry alternating current (AC) that generates magnetic fields |
| **Audio** | Desk‑mic cable, XLR or USB‑C audio interface, speaker wires, headphone cable | Carry low‑level analog signals that are vulnerable to interference |
| **Video** | HDMI, DisplayPort, USB‑C video cables | Can act as antennas for stray EM fields |

Draw a simple sketch of your desk top view, marking where each cable enters the workspace. Identify the **power zone** (usually near the power strip) and the **audio zone** (often around the mic stand or interface). The goal is to keep these zones at least a few inches apart and, if possible, on different vertical levels.

## 2. Choose the Right Length and Quantity

Nettbe offers two length options:

- **6‑inch** ties – perfect for bundling a short run of power cords or a tight mic‑to‑interface cable.
- **8‑inch** ties – give a little extra slack for longer runs or for wrapping around a small power strip.

Pick a pack size that matches the scale of your setup. A **pack of 60** works for a minimalist single‑mic desk, while a **126‑piece** or **210‑piece** pack is more comfortable for dual‑monitor rigs with multiple speakers and a separate audio interface.

## 3. Separate Power and Audio Paths

### a. Create a Dedicated Power Channel

1. **Mount a small cable tray or adhesive channel** along the back edge of your desk, directly under the power strip.  
2. Run all AC adapters, charger cords, and monitor power cables through this channel.  
3. Keep the channel as flat as possible; a low‑profile path reduces the chance of the magnetic field coupling into nearby audio wires.

### b. Route Audio Cables on the Opposite Side

1. **Place a second channel** (or simply use the desk surface) on the opposite side of the power channel.  
2. Run the mic cable, speaker wires, and any video cables here. If you have a monitor arm, route its cables along the arm’s built‑in cable management clip, which naturally separates them from the power zone.

### c. Use Vertical Separation When Possible

If your desk has a modest height, run the power channel **under the desk** and the audio channel **on top of the desk**. Even a one‑inch vertical gap can cut EMI dramatically because the magnetic field strength drops off quickly with distance.

## 4. Secure Bundles with Nettbe Reusable Cable Ties

### Step‑by‑Step Tying

1. **Gather a bundle** of power cords that belong together (e.g., all monitor adapters).  
2. **Wrap a 6‑inch tie** around the middle of the bundle. The hook‑and‑loop surface lets you slide the tie tighter or looser without cutting.  
3. **Pull the tail** of the tie until it sits snugly against the bundle, then press the Velcro strips together. The tie stays in place but can be opened later for adjustments.  
4. **Repeat** for each group of audio cables, using an 8‑inch tie if the bundle is longer.

Because the ties are made of **nylon**, they are lightweight yet sturdy enough to hold multiple cords without slipping. The reusable nature means you can re‑route cables later—ideal for a hybrid work‑from‑home schedule where you frequently unplug and re‑plug devices.

### Color‑Coding (Optional)

If you want an extra visual cue, you can buy colored hook‑and‑loop ties from the same manufacturer or use a small piece of colored tape on the tie’s tail. This makes it easy to spot a power bundle versus an audio bundle at a glance, which speeds up troubleshooting later.

## 5. Test for Hum Reduction

After you’ve separated and tied everything, plug in your equipment and do a quick audio check:

1. Open your DAW or recording software and enable a test tone on the mic.  
2. Listen for any low‑frequency hum.  
3. If you still hear noise, try moving the audio bundle a few more inches away from the power channel or add a **ground loop isolator** on the audio line.

Most users report a noticeable drop in hum after the first round of separation. The effect is especially pronounced when the power cords are routed through a metal‑backed tray, which acts as a shield.

## 6. Maintain the Setup

Because hook‑and‑loop ties can collect dust over time, give them a quick wipe with a dry cloth every few months. If you notice the Velcro losing its grip, replace the affected ties—Nettbe’s packs are inexpensive enough to keep a spare on hand.

## 7. When to Upgrade Your Cable Management

If you find yourself constantly adding new peripherals, consider a **modular cable management system** that incorporates both trays and Velcro ties. This allows you to expand the power zone without crowding the audio zone. For larger studios, a dedicated **rack‑mount power conditioner** placed on a separate shelf can provide even more physical distance between power and signal paths.

## Potential Drawbacks

- **Hook‑and‑loop can wear**: Repeated opening and closing may reduce the adhesive strength of the Velcro over many years. For a permanent, high‑stress bundle (like a heavy‑duty power strip), a single‑use zip tie might hold longer, though it sacrifices reusability.
- **Limited thickness capacity**: Very thick power cords (e.g., industrial‑grade IEC cables) may be too bulky for a standard 6‑inch or 8‑inch tie to secure tightly. In those cases, a larger cable clamp or a dedicated cable sleeve works better.
