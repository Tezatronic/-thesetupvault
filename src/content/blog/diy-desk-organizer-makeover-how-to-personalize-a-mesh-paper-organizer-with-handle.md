## Step 1: Define Your Zones

Before you reach for paint or tape, decide what each of the five trays will hold. Typical zones include:

| Tray | Suggested Use |
|------|----------------|
| 1 | Incoming paperwork (invoices, mail) |
| 2 | Ongoing projects (drafts, reference sheets) |
| 3 | Completed items (to‑file, archive) |
| 4 | Office supplies (sticky notes, pens) |
| 5 | Personal items (notes, small gadgets) |

Write these categories on a sheet of paper and keep it nearby. Having a clear plan prevents you from later adding a color that doesn’t match its purpose.

## Step 2: Choose a Color‑Coding System

Color works best when it’s consistent across your whole workspace. Pick a palette that already appears in your desk accessories—perhaps the blue of your mouse pad, the green of a plant pot, or the orange of a lamp shade. Limit yourself to **four to five colors** so the system stays easy to read.

**Materials you’ll need**

- Washable acrylic paint or spray paint (choose matte finish to avoid glare)
- Painter’s tape or masking tape
- Small paintbrushes or a foam roller
- Clear sealant (optional, for durability)

### Applying the paint

1. **Mask the edges** – Use tape to protect the metal frame where you don’t want paint.
2. **Paint each tray** – Apply a thin, even coat. Because the mesh is porous, a second light coat may be necessary after the first dries.
3. **Seal** – If you expect heavy use, a quick spray of clear sealant will keep the color from chipping.

Allow the paint to dry completely (usually 2–4 hours) before moving on.

## Step 3: Add Labels That Pop

Labels are the backbone of any color‑coded system. For a clean look, use a label maker or printable adhesive labels. If you prefer a more handcrafted vibe, hand‑lettered tags work well.

**Tips for effective labeling**

- **Use the same font size** across all trays for visual harmony.
- **Include both a word and an icon** (e.g., a tiny envelope for “Incoming”) to aid quick scanning.
- **Laminate or cover** the labels with clear tape if you expect them to get wet or smudged.

If you already use cable‑management ties, you can repurpose the same color palette for your labels, tying the whole desk aesthetic together. The article on [step‑by‑step cable labeling & color‑coding with Nettbe reusable ties](/blog/step-by-step-cable-labeling-color-coding-with-nettbe-reusable-ties/) offers a great reference for matching colors.

## Step 4: Attach Small Accessories

The built‑in handle makes the organizer portable, but you can also turn each tray into a mini‑station.

- **Magnetic clips** – Stick a small strip of magnetic tape to the inside of a tray; it holds paper clips, thumbtacks, or a tiny notepad.
- **Mini‑pockets** – Sew or glue a fabric pocket to the front of a tray for receipts or business cards.
- **LED strip** – A thin, battery‑operated LED strip along the back edge adds subtle illumination, making the colored trays easier to see in low light.

These tweaks don’t require drilling or permanent changes, so you can rearrange them later.

## Step 5: Test the Flow

Place the organizer on your desk and run through a typical workday routine:

1. Drop a new invoice into the “Incoming” tray.
2. Move a draft to “Ongoing” after you start working on it.
3. When finished, slide it to “Completed.”

If any tray feels out of place, adjust the color or label. The goal is a seamless visual cue that lets you file without thinking.

## Step 6: Document Your Setup

Take a quick photo of the finished organizer and note the color‑to‑function mapping. Save the image in a folder called “Desk Systems” alongside any other workspace photos. This documentation makes it easy to replicate the system if you ever need a new organizer or want to share the setup with a teammate.

## Potential Drawbacks

- **Paint wear over time** – Mesh can be abrasive; if you frequently slide heavy files, the paint may chip. A clear sealant helps, but you may need touch‑ups after several months.
- **Limited storage depth** – Because the trays are open‑front and relatively shallow, very thick bundles of paper can spill over. For large files, consider a separate drawer unit.
