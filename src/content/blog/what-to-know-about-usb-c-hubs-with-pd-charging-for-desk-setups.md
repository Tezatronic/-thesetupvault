## Downstream PD Charging vs. Host Passthrough Power

USB Power Delivery (USB PD) is a protocol that negotiates voltage and current levels up to specific wattage profiles between a power source and a receiver. In compact travel docks, PD ports are typically "passthrough" inputs: you plug your laptop's existing charger into the hub, the hub siphons off 10 to 15 watts to run its own internal controllers, and it passes the remaining wattage through a single tethered cable to your laptop.

A dedicated desktop USB hub with PD charging takes a fundamentally different architectural approach. Instead of leeching power from your computer or relying on your laptop's power adapter, it uses an external power supply plugged directly into the wall. 

In this setup, the integrated USB-C PD ports act as downstream power stations. Rather than powering your laptop, these ports deliver targeted fast-charging power directly to your secondary workflow devices—such as an iPad, an Android tablet, a smartphone, or a wireless headphone case. Your laptop connects via a dedicated data host line, while your daily mobile gear charges within arm's reach on your desk without competing for your computer's limited internal bus power.

Using an external power brick keeps your computer's Thunderbolt or USB-C ports from being overloaded. If you frequently connect external drives, audio interfaces, and charging cables simultaneously, relying entirely on bus power often leads to random drive disconnects and throttled charging speeds. 

## Key Factors When Choosing a USB-C Hub with PD Charging

Selecting the right USB C hub multiport device requires balancing electrical delivery, data bandwidth, and port placement.

### 1. Power Distribution and Dedicated Wattage
Check how the hub allocates its total available wattage. If a hub comes with an external power supply, that total pool of wattage must supply the internal chipset, the standard data ports (which typically provide standard 5V bus power to flash drives and mice), and any high-output PD ports. 

Look for models that clearly segregate their power lines. When high-wattage charging ports operate on independent circuits, plugging a dead tablet into a fast-charge port will not cause an external SSD connected on an adjacent data port to reset.

### 2. Data Bandwidth Split (10Gbps vs. 5Gbps)
Not all USB ports handle the same data rates. USB 3.0 (also labeled USB 3.1 Gen 1 or USB 3.2 Gen 1) caps data transfer speeds at 5Gbps. For keyboards, mice, and standard desktop accessories, 5Gbps is more than enough bandwidth.

However, if you move video footage, large photo libraries, or virtual machine images to external drives, look for USB 3.2 Gen 2 ports that support transfer speeds up to 10Gbps. Having a mix of both port generations ensures you do not waste a high-bandwidth 10Gbps connection on a simple peripheral like a [solid 1080p webcam](/blog/the-best-1080p-webcam-with-microphone-for-zoom-and-teams-calls/), while still keeping high-speed lanes open for external NVMe enclosures.

### 3. Port Geometry and Desk Ergonomics
Port orientation determines how messy your workstation looks. Flat dongles that sit loosely behind a computer tend to slide around whenever a thick cable is attached. Desktop hubs designed with weighted bases or angled front faces allow you to insert flash drives and charging cables with one hand, without lifting or shifting the entire hub across your desk pad. Managing the resulting bundle with [reusable cable ties for desk cable management](/blog/the-best-reusable-cable-ties-for-desk-cable-management-a-practical-buying-guide/) keeps your workspace clean.
